About Kheya

A considered way
from idea to software.

Kheya helps business and product teams decide what to change, build the software they need, and test whether it works in practice. Start with a workflow, a system, or a technology decision.

The idea behind the name

A crossing, taken one step at a time

Our name is borrowed from khēyā (খেয়া), Bengali for a ferry crossing, or “ferrying across”. It is also the title of Rabindranath Tagore’s 1906 book of Bengali poems, Kheya.

A ferry takes people across a gap they cannot cross alone. That is how we think about change: know where you are, choose where to land, and steer a steady course when conditions shift.

Our philosophy

Explore. Choose. Make it useful.

Divergence opens up the possibilities. Convergence gives them a direction. Emergence tests what the pieces can do together.

Point at a ringed dot or a focus area.

What could help?

Map the work, listen to the people doing it, and explore where software could help. Start with the problem before choosing a tool.

What should we try first?

Compare value, feasibility, and risk. Choose a focused use case, agree what success means, and make the trade-offs clear.

What works together?

Build and test in the real workflow. Agree operational ownership and review needs. Learn from the result before deciding what to improve or extend.

Workflows: Map the recurring work and where effort is repeated.

Data: Understand what information exists and who can access it.

People: Listen to the people doing the work and using its results.

Friction: Find delays, manual handoffs and questions that are hard to answer.

Tools: Review the systems already in use before adding another one.

Opportunities: Connect possible software uses to a specific business problem.

Value: Ask which outcome would make a useful difference to the team.

Feasibility: Check the data, tools and effort needed to make the idea work.

Risk: Identify what could go wrong and where a person should stay involved.

Scope: Keep the first project small enough to test and learn from.

Success: Agree how to judge the result before starting the build.

Starting point: Choose one use case with clear trade-offs and next steps.

Build: Connect the chosen approach to the real workflow.

Test: Evaluate the result against the success criteria you agreed.

Oversight: Keep review and escalation clear when the system needs a person.

Ownership: Make it clear who operates the system and handles problems.

Monitoring: Agree who monitors quality, reliability and cost; this does not establish ongoing support.

Improvement: Use feedback and measured results to decide what to change next.

An illustrative workflow

A team copies approved requests between two tools. Here is how the approach could apply.

  1. Explore the handoff. Look at the steps and exceptions, including whether a process change would help.
  2. Choose one request type. Use an approved connection, keep approval with a person and agree checks for correct transfer and visible failures.
  3. Test the complete task. Review it with users, including exceptions, then decide whether to refine or extend it.

This explains the approach; it is not a client story or a measured result.

Working principles

What shapes the work

Practical commitments for decisions, access and ownership throughout an engagement.

01

Start with real work

Listen to the people doing the work and respect their constraints. A simpler process may be the better answer.

02

Make assumptions visible

Record assumptions, dependencies and unresolved questions so the people making decisions can review them.

03

Keep people in control

Define what the system may do, when a person must approve it, and how to stop or recover when something goes wrong.

04

Design for handover

Agree documentation and operating responsibilities for the work delivered. Further support needs its own scope.
